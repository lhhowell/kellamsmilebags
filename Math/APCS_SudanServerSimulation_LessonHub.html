Why This Lesson Works
The Triple-Threat Lesson
One lab. Four learning targets. A standards-aligned bridge between technical content and ethical reasoning.

The Sudan Server Simulation hits all four of the unit's learning targets simultaneously. Rather than teaching each in isolation, the lab lets students experience the technical concepts (handshake, ACKs, retransmission) and then immediately confront their ethical implications (the Digital Divide).

How the Lab Hits Each Target

Devices Working Together in a Network

Students see that the "Web Server" and the "Client" aren't just sending files — they're in a constant, synchronized dialogue. If the Server doesn't hear from the Client (the ACK), it stops working. Networking is cooperation, not just transmission.

How the Internet Actually Works

By acting out the Three-Way Handshake and Retransmission, students move past the "Internet as a cloud" mental model and see it as a series of specific, rigid steps: SYN, ACK, Checksum. The machinery becomes visible.

The Digital Divide, Beyond Access

The core of the simulation. Students learn that the Digital Divide isn't just about "having a computer" — it's about latency. The same protocol that works perfectly in a high-speed city becomes a barrier to entry in a high-latency region.

Ethical Concerns in Computing

The closing question: if our global standards (TCP) are designed for high-speed infrastructure, are we accidentally locking out developing nations from the modern economy? Students see the bias built into technical protocols.

The pedagogical move The lesson uses an AI-generated analogy (the "Lunch Line Protocol") that is deliberately flawed as the entry point. Students audit it, find the errors, and then run a real simulation. This positions AI as a tool to interrogate, not a tool to obey — which is the exact disposition AP CSP wants students to develop toward computing innovations generally.

Why 60 Minutes?

The source curriculum (Mobile CSP Unit 6.2–6.3) allocates 1.5 blocks (~135 minutes) for this content. The AI-integrated version condenses it to one 60-minute block because the Gemini role-play accomplishes in 20 minutes what a static reading + worksheet would take 40+ minutes to achieve. Students feel the latency rather than reading about it.

What's Different About This Version

  • The simulation is experiential, not abstract. The Gemini server makes students wait. The wait is the point.
  • The AI is a partner, not an answer key. Students audit its first explanation and then use it to model a system. They are positioned as auditors, not consumers.
  • The Digital Divide is named, not implied. Omar's story (next pages) gives the divide a face. Ethics moves from abstract to concrete in one prompt.

The Lunch Line Audit
Find what the AI got wrong about how the Internet actually works.

Learning Target

I can analyze a technical analogy to identify inaccuracies in how computing devices work together in a network.

The Scenario

An AI was asked to explain the TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) using a high school cafeteria analogy. The result reads smoothly — but the AI simplified the rules of the Internet too much. Your job is to act as a Lead Network Engineer and audit the analogy for technical inaccuracies.

The Analogy: The "Lunch Line" Protocol

The AI's explanation The Handshake. In the cafeteria, stepping into the back of the line is the handshake. By joining the line, you signal: "I am ready to receive data (pizza)."

Error Recovery. If the lunch lady tells you, "We're out of chocolate milk," you decide to wait for a new batch or choose an alternative (like water).

Acknowledgment. The cashier scans your ID, you get a receipt at the end. This confirms the transaction is complete.

Find the Flaws

Locate three technical inaccuracies in the analogy above. For each, write what the analogy says, what the technical reality is, and your correction.

  1. The Handshake Why is "joining the line" not a complete handshake? (Hint: think about the three-way conversation between SYN, SYN-ACK, and ACK.) Write your correction below.
  2. Error Recovery Does TCP let you swap data if a packet is lost? What does it actually do? Write your correction below.
  3. Acknowledgment When do ACKs actually happen — at the end of a transaction, or somewhere else? Write your correction below.
Why this matters If we used this AI's "Lunch Line" rules to send a photo of your cat to a friend, and the cat's ear was lost in transit — what would the final photo look like under the AI's Error Recovery rule? (Hint: a cat with a glass of water where its ear should be.)

Next Step

Keep this sheet on your desk. You'll need your corrections to survive the Sudan Server Simulation coming up next.

The Sudan Server Simulation
Feel the latency. Track the wait. Audit the protocol.

Learning Target

I can explain how computing devices work together in a network and how latency affects communication.

Goal

Experience how the reliability of TCP becomes a hurdle in a high-latency environment. You will role-play a client trying to download a 5-slice pizza from a server in Sudan, with 2000ms of latency on every step.

Copy this prompt into Gemini
Act as a web server in Sudan with a high Latency of 2000ms. I am trying to "download" a 5-slice pizza using the TCP Protocol. For every slice of pizza you "send" me, stop and wait for me to type "ACK" before you send the next one. Also, for slice #3, pretend it was "lost" (Packet Loss) and tell me I have to ask for it again. Start the simulation now by initiating the Three-Way Handshake (SYN).
→ Paste into Gemini, then follow the steps below.

Lab Steps

01
Step
The Handshake (The "Hello" Delay)

Gemini sends you a SYN. Reply with SYN-ACK. Count to 4 slowly after you hit enter — that represents the time your message takes to reach Sudan and the server's response to come back.

Your move:
SYN-ACK
02
Step
The Data Stream (Slices 1 & 2)

Gemini sends Slice #1 and waits. Reply with ACK. The protocol forces the server to stop for every single slice — this is Flow Control.

Your move:
ACK
03
Step
The Packet Loss (The "Black Hole")

Gemini sends Slice #3, then tells you it was lost in transit. You cannot ACK what you didn't receive. You must request retransmission. In Omar's world, this single lost packet adds seconds of waiting.

Your move:
Requesting Retransmission of Slice #3
04
Step
The Finish Line (Slices 4 & 5)

Complete the download. Look at the clock. A 5-slice pizza took nearly a minute. A real website has hundreds of slices.

Reflection — The "Audit"

  1. The Time Cost How many seconds were "wasted" just waiting for messages to travel back and forth? What part of that wait is latency, and what part is the protocol's design?
  2. TCP vs. UDP If you were using UDP (no acknowledgments) instead of TCP, how would the Slice #3 situation have been handled differently? What would the trade-off be?
  3. The Divide Why does a reliable protocol like TCP make the Internet feel broken for a student in Sudan, rather than just "slow"?

The Ethical Brief
Translate the technical experience into ethical reasoning.

Learning Target

I can explain how the use of computing can raise legal and ethical concerns, specifically regarding the Digital Divide.

The Scenario

Meet Omar Omar is a high school student in Sudan taking a timed SAT prep test online. The test software is built using TCP. Every time he clicks "Next Question," the server waits for an ACK to confirm he received the data. Because his connection has high latency and packets are occasionally lost, his test times out and locks him out before he can finish.

He worked toward this for months. The page just refreshed.

Your Task

Write a structured response to the question below. Use at least three technical terms from the vocabulary list (Latency, TCP, ACK, Retransmission, Handshake, Packet Loss). Reference specific data from your Simulation Log.

The question In Omar's case, is this a failure of his computer or local internet — or is it an ethical failure in how the test's digital rules were designed? Who is responsible for fixing this Digital Divide?

Brief Structure (suggested)

  1. Open with the technical reality. What's actually happening to Omar in TCP terms? (Use 2–3 vocabulary terms here.)
  2. Cite the simulation. Reference a specific time from your Simulation Log to show why this isn't a small inconvenience.
  3. Make the ethical move. Whose responsibility is the failure — Omar's, his ISP's, the test designer's, or the protocol designers'? Defend your answer.
  4. Propose. What would a fairer system look like? (Different protocol? Different test design? Different infrastructure investment?)

What Excellence Looks Like

  • Uses three or more technical terms correctly to explain causation
  • Connects the protocol's standardization to the unfairness — TCP works the same everywhere, which is precisely the problem
  • References a specific number from the Simulation Log as evidence
  • Names a clear party as responsible — and explains why

Closing Reflection (5 minutes)

Pair-share one insight from your brief with a partner. Be ready to share with the class: "The thing the simulation taught me that I didn't get from the reading was…"

Grading Criteria
Rubrics
Two rubrics — one for the AI Audit, one for the Ethical Brief. Designed to differentiate the student who copied a definition from the one who understands the logic of the protocol.
Rubric 1 · The AI Audit (Part 1)
Concept Developing · 1 pt Proficient · 2 pts Exemplary Auditor · 3 pts
The Handshake Says the AI is wrong because it's not just "standing in line." Mentions that it requires a "back-and-forth" conversation to start. Specifically identifies the SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK sequence. Explains that without this, the connection isn't established.
Error Recovery Says you don't "pick a different drink" if the milk is gone. Explains that the server has to send the missing data again. Uses the term Retransmission. Explains that TCP is "reliable" and cannot move to the next slice until the missing/corrupted one is fixed.
Acknowledgment Says the receipt shouldn't be at the very end. Explains that the student has to "nod" or "confirm" after every few bites. Connects ACKs to Flow Control. Explains that if the server doesn't get an ACK, it stops sending (as seen in the Sudan simulation).
Rubric 2 · The Ethical Brief (Part 3)
Criteria Beginning · 1 pt Developing · 2 pts Proficient · 3 pts
Technical Integration Mentions the internet is "slow" but uses no technical networking terms. Uses 1–2 terms correctly but lacks a full explanation of the "wait time." Uses 3+ terms correctly to explain why the "Time Out" happened (e.g., Latency + ACKs).
Ethical Reasoning States that Omar just needs better internet or a faster computer. Identifies that the situation is unfair but doesn't connect it to the design of the software. Explains how standardized protocols create an unfair barrier for users in low-infrastructure areas.
Simulation Evidence Does not reference the simulation experience. Mentions that the simulation was slow but provides no specific data. References specific data from the Lab Log (e.g., "It took 26 seconds for 5 slices").
Scoring note The "Exemplary Auditor" column on the AI Audit rubric distinguishes the student who copied vocabulary from the one who understands the logic of why the protocol is designed that way. Look for explanations that connect WHY a TCP rule exists (reliability, ordering, flow control) — not just naming the rule.

PBA Extension · Optional
Performance-Based Assessment · Extension Project
Omar's Voice
Omar's story is one story. Your students each find another — a high school student in a different country with their own version of the infrastructure hike, the timed-out heartbreak, the digital ghost hunt. The PBA invites them to learn one voice and tell us what they hear.

The Driving Question

How does a country's digital infrastructure shape the daily life of a teenager trying to participate in the global online economy — and what would a more just protocol look like?

Student Deliverables

  • A persona narrative (~500 words). Student picks a country with a documented digital divide (Sudan, Yemen, Madagascar, rural Bolivia, etc.) and writes a first-person account from a teenager there — modeled on Omar's voice. Generated with Gemini's help, then verified against real infrastructure data.
  • An infrastructure data brief (1 page). Internet penetration rate, dominant connection type (2G/3G/4G/5G), average latency, average cost per GB as a percent of household income. Cited sources.
  • An ethical argument (~750 words). Whose responsibility is the divide? Protocol designers? Governments? International bodies? Tech companies? The student takes a defensible position.
  • A protocol proposal (1 page + diagram). What would a "Digital Divide–Aware" protocol look like? Could be a real one (HTTP/3, QUIC) or an imagined modification.

The Four Phases

Phase 1 · Week 1
Voice
Choose a country. Generate persona narrative with Gemini. Critique it for stereotyping or inaccuracy.
Phase 2 · Week 1–2
Data
Research real infrastructure conditions. Verify or revise the narrative against actual data.
Phase 3 · Week 2
Argument
Develop and defend an ethical position on responsibility for the Digital Divide.
Phase 4 · Week 3
Proposal
Design or research a protocol modification that would reduce the barrier. Present to the class.

The AI Move

The PBA teaches students to use AI responsibly — as a draft partner whose output must be verified, not consumed. Phase 1 produces a Gemini-drafted persona. Phase 2 forces students to fact-check it. The gap between what Gemini imagined and what the data shows becomes part of the ethical argument in Phase 3.

Connecting to AP CSP standards This PBA aligns to Big Idea 5 (Impact of Computing) and supports the Create Performance Task's emphasis on a student-developed computational artifact. The protocol proposal in Phase 4 satisfies the artifact requirement; the ethical argument satisfies the impact requirement.

Why This Extension Matters

The 60-minute lesson gives students a vivid, embodied understanding of latency and packet loss. The PBA gives them time — time to learn one country deeply, sit with one voice, and develop a defensible position on a real ethical question. The simulation lit the fire. The PBA lets it become a craft.
